# Modern (deep-learning and LLM era) product recommendation from purchase / browsing history, and managed "recommendations as a service" — state as of October 2026

Reader context: Django + PostgreSQL + Redis shop, no ML infrastructure, no GPU, cron batch jobs, tens of thousands of products, modest order volume, product texts in Russian and Uzbek. Every section separates "works with little data" from "needs big data and an ML team". Classic association rules / item-item CF / matrix factorization are mentioned only as baselines (covered by another researcher).

Source-quality legend used below: **[primary]** = paper, official docs or official pricing page fetched in this session; **[secondary]** = third-party summary / aggregator, treat as indicative only.

## 1. Item embeddings learned from baskets / sessions: item2vec, prod2vec, Meta-Prod2Vec

### Takeaway
These are word2vec (skip-gram with negative sampling) applied to "sentences" of items (a basket, a session, a user's purchase sequence): cheap to train on CPU, but the published wins come from very large datasets, and on frequent item pairs plain co-occurrence counts are as good or better — embeddings only add value for the long tail and unseen pairs, and only if hyperparameters are tuned for recommendation. For a small shop they are an optional second step after co-occurrence counts, not a replacement.

### Cited Findings
- **item2vec** (Barkan & Koenigstein, 2016) casts item-based collaborative filtering in the word-embedding framework: skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS) learns a latent vector per item; it infers item-item relations without any user information, and the paper's own claim is only that it is "competitive with SVD" (not that it beats it). [primary] — [arXiv 1603.04259](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.04259)
- **prod2vec** ("E-commerce in Your Inbox", Grbovic et al., KDD 2015) learned product vectors from purchase sequences extracted from e-mail receipts of **more than 29 million users across 172 e-commerce sites**; baselines were popular products and co-occurrence-predicted products; online it reported a steady **9% CTR lift** over other ad formats in Yahoo Mail (launched for the 2014 holiday season). This is a "big data" result, not a small-shop result. [primary] — [arXiv 1606.07154](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07154)
- **Meta-Prod2Vec** (Vasile, Smirnova, Conneau; RecSys 2016, Criteo) adds item metadata (categories, brands, tags) as side information that regularizes the item embeddings, motivated by the fact that prod2vec uses only co-occurrence and therefore gives poor vectors to products with zero or few co-occurrences. [primary] — [arXiv 1607.07326](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.07326); [Criteo AI Lab page](https://ailab.criteo.com/meta-prod2vec-product-embeddings-using-side-information-recommendation/)
- Meta-Prod2Vec evaluation: 30Music dataset (≈100k user sessions, ≈433k songs, ≈67k artists); compared BestOf (popularity), **CoCounts (co-occurrence cosine)**, Prod2Vec, Meta-Prod2Vec and ensembles on HR@20 / NDCG@20. Reported pattern: standalone Meta-Prod2Vec is clearly best when the (query, target) pair has **zero co-occurrences** in training; the **ensemble of Meta-Prod2Vec + CoCounts** is best for pairs with low-but-nonzero counts; the authors' wording is that CoCounts "memorizes" frequent pairs while Meta-Prod2Vec "helps to generalize on unseen ones". [primary, read via search summary of the paper] — [arXiv 1607.07326](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.07326)
- **BB2vec** ("Inferring Complementary Products from Baskets and Browsing Sessions", 2018) combines basket and browsing data for complementary-item embeddings; it reports that its advantage over prod2vec-type models is largest on **sparse datasets** and for **products with few or no purchases** — i.e. browsing sessions compensate for missing purchase co-occurrence. [primary, via search summary] — [arXiv 1809.09621](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.09621)
- "Word2vec applied to Recommendation: Hyperparameters Matter" (Caselles-Dupré, Lesaint, Royo-Letelier, RecSys 2018): the NLP default hyperparameters are wrong for recommendation; negative-sampling distribution, number of epochs, subsampling and window size matter most, and tuning them "can increase [performance] by an order of magnitude". [primary] — [arXiv 1804.04212](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.04212)
- Broader reproducibility context: Dacrema, Cremonesi, Jannach (RecSys 2019) examined 18 neural top-n recommendation algorithms from top conferences; only 7 were reproducible with reasonable effort, and **6 of those 7 could often be outperformed by simple nearest-neighbour or graph-based heuristics**; the 7th did not consistently beat a well-tuned non-neural linear method. [primary] — [arXiv 1907.06902](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06902)
- Eugene Yan lists aggregated co-interaction statistics (item-to-item similarity, e.g. Alibaba's Swing) as the non-ML baseline to build before any learned model. [primary: practitioner blog] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/first-rule-of-ml](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/first-rule-of-ml/)

### Inferences
- Data requirement: training itself is trivial (gensim word2vec on CPU, minutes for tens of thousands of baskets), but the *signal* requirement is the same as for co-occurrence: an item needs to appear in many baskets/sessions to get a meaningful vector. With "modest order volume" most of a building-materials catalog will be in the tail where neither approach has signal; Meta-Prod2Vec's lesson is that the fix for the tail is **metadata / content**, not a cleverer co-occurrence model.
- No source found shows item2vec/prod2vec beating co-occurrence counts on a *small* dataset. The evidence is consistent with: co-occurrence counts for head items + content similarity (category, attributes, text embeddings — section 4) for tail items is the small-data equivalent of the "Meta-Prod2Vec + CoCounts ensemble".
- Browsing sessions (product views, cart adds) are a much larger signal source than orders (BB2vec); for a small shop, logging views per session is likely worth more than a better algorithm on orders alone.
- If embeddings are tried, default word2vec hyperparameters must not be used (Caselles-Dupré): many epochs, tuned negative-sampling exponent, window = whole basket.

### Gaps
- No direct published head-to-head of item2vec vs. simple co-occurrence on datasets with < 100k baskets was found; the "embeddings do not help on small data" conclusion is inferred from the cited papers, not directly measured.
- The exact datasets and numbers of the item2vec paper were not retrieved (only the abstract was read).
- Exact Meta-Prod2Vec table values were not verified from the PDF (the slide-based numbers returned by search were inconsistent and are deliberately not reported).

## 2. Sequential and session-based recommendation: GRU4Rec, SASRec, BERT4Rec vs. session-KNN (S-KNN, V-SKNN, STAN, VSTAN)

### Takeaway
Independent comparison studies from 2018 through 2024 repeatedly find that tuned nearest-neighbour session methods (S-KNN, V-SKNN, STAN/VSTAN) and simple sequential rules match or beat neural session models (GRU4Rec, NARM, STAMP, SR-GNN, newer GNNs) on small and mid-size e-commerce datasets, while training in seconds instead of hours. Neural sequential models are an option for teams with large logs and ML engineers; a small shop should start with session-KNN / sequential rules.

### Cited Findings
- **Ludewig & Jannach 2018** ("Evaluation of Session-based Recommendation Algorithms") compared GRU4Rec and factorized Markov models with simple nearest-neighbour schemes and found the nearest-neighbour class "despite its sometimes almost trivial nature" performs as well as or significantly better than the neural approaches in many cases. [primary] — [arXiv 1803.09587](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09587)
- **Ludewig, Mauro, Latifi, Jannach** ("Empirical Analysis of Session-Based Recommendation Algorithms", arXiv 2019, journal version UMUAI DOI 10.1007/s11257-020-09277-1) compared 12 approaches under identical conditions — non-neural: AR (association rules), SR (sequential rules), SKNN/V-SKNN, STAN, VSTAN, CT; neural: GRU4Rec, NARM, STAMP, NextItNet, SR-GNN, CSRM. Conclusion: accuracy gains of neural methods are "still limited"; "in most cases" simple nearest-neighbour heuristics are preferable. [primary] — [arXiv 1910.12781](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12781); full text [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.12781)
- Dataset sizes in that study are relevant to a small shop — two of the e-commerce datasets are small: **RETAIL (210k actions, 60k sessions, 32k items)** and **DIGI (264k actions, 55k sessions, 32k items)**; others: RSC15 (5.4M actions, 1.4M sessions, 29k items), ZALANDO (4.5M actions, 365k sessions, 189k items). [primary] — [ar5iv 1910.12781](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.12781)
- Best method per dataset in that study (tables 6–7): HR@20 — STAN on RETAIL (0.5938), VSTAN on DIGI (0.4803), ZALANDO (0.5362) and RSC15 (0.6761); **neural methods won HR@20 on none of the eight datasets**; MRR@20 was won by a neural method on only three (GRU4Rec on ZALANDO, SR-GNN on RSC15, NARM on 8TRACKS). Note: the page reader flagged an internal inconsistency for RETAIL MRR (text credits SR-GNN, table lists STAN) — check the PDF before quoting that single cell. [primary] — [ar5iv 1910.12781](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.12781)
- Cost: on ZALANDO, SR-GNN needed **1527 minutes** of training vs. **0.13 minutes** for VSTAN ("several orders of magnitude more time"); several neural methods and CT "exhibit major scalability issues when the number of recommendable items increases" (NextItNet ran out of memory on four datasets); kNN prediction times "usually lie within the time constraints of real-time recommendation". [primary] — [ar5iv 1910.12781](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.12781)
- User study in the same paper (N=250, online radio): SKNN recommendations were rated significantly higher than GRU4Rec and AR — the heuristic's advantage is not only an offline artefact. [primary] — [ar5iv 1910.12781](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.12781)
- RecSys 2019 version ("Performance Comparison of Neural and Non-Neural Approaches to Session-based Recommendation"): in the majority of cases simple techniques outperformed four recent neural approaches; the authors attribute the "non-progress" to weak or untuned baselines in the original papers. [primary] — [iris.unito.it record](https://iris.unito.it/handle/2318/1712206)
- **Later replication (GNN era)**: Shehzad & Jannach, "Performance Comparison of Session-based Recommendation Algorithms based on GNNs" (ECIR 2024) re-ran eight recent GNN session models under one protocol on three datasets: kNN and sequential-rule baselines beat **every** GNN model on MRR; GNNs won only three cases on Hit Rate; random seeds alone noticeably changed GNN results. [primary, abstract level] — [arXiv 2312.16695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16695)
- **BERT4Rec vs. SASRec**: Petrov & Macdonald (RecSys 2022) found BERT4Rec is not systematically better than SASRec across the published literature and that the original BERT4Rec results could not be reproduced with the default configurations of available implementations. [primary] — [arXiv 2207.07483](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07483)
- Klenitskiy & Vasilev (RecSys 2023): when both are trained with the same loss (full-softmax cross-entropy), **SASRec significantly outperforms BERT4Rec in quality and training speed**; SASRec with negative sampling also wins if the number of negatives is much larger than one. I.e. reported gains were a loss-function artefact, not an architecture win. [primary] — [arXiv 2309.07602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07602)
- **Home-improvement case study** (Kouki et al., RecSys 2020, authors from RelationalAI / The Home Depot): 14 session-based algorithms, 8 metrics, 3 datasets — **no clear winner across metrics**; standard metrics were judged ill-suited because they only reward predicting the exact clicked/purchased item ID while the catalog has near-identical products under different IDs; human experts' opinions on the top-5 algorithms "often" differed from the offline ranking. [primary: abstract] — [RelationalAI page](https://relational.ai/blog/from-the-lab-to-production-a-case-study-of-session-based-recommendations-in-the-home-improvement). A secondary source states that the neural approach finally deployed gave a significant KPI increase over an existing commercial recommendation product. [secondary] — [Recommender Systems: A Primer, arXiv 2302.02579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.02579)
- Scaling-law evidence that transformers need data: for decoder-only sequential recommenders (98.3K to 0.8B parameters) loss follows a power law in model size; a 98.3K-parameter model needed ~18.5M interactions to match a 75.5M-parameter model trained on 9.2M — i.e. the regime where big sequential models pay off is tens of millions of interactions. [secondary summary of the paper by a practitioner] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/)

### Inferences
- "Works for a small shop": S-KNN / V-SKNN / STAN / VSTAN and sequential rules. They need only a table of (session_id, item_id, timestamp), no training step beyond indexing recent sessions, no GPU, and they were the best or near-best on datasets the size of a small shop (55k–60k sessions, ~32k items). They can be implemented in SQL/Python as a cron job that precomputes "next items" per item or computed on the fly from Redis.
- "Needs big data and an ML team": GRU4Rec, SASRec, BERT4Rec, SR-GNN and newer GNN models — GPU training, hyperparameter search, sensitivity to implementation details and seeds; no independent study shows them reliably beating tuned kNN on small e-commerce logs.
- The Home Depot observation matters for building materials specifically: many near-duplicate SKUs (same product in different sizes/colours) make exact-item offline metrics misleading; evaluate at the level of variant group / item category and include a manual expert review of recommendation lists before launch.
- Purchase sessions in building materials are project-driven and sparse; the session-KNN family uses *views and cart adds within a session*, so it depends on logging browsing events, not only orders.

### Gaps
- The full Kouki et al. paper (which 14 algorithms, dataset sizes, the deployed algorithm, exact KPI lift) could not be retrieved — only the abstract and one secondary mention; the "neural approach won in production at Home Depot" claim is therefore secondary and should be worded cautiously. Home Depot's data scale is also far beyond a small shop.
- No comparison study was found on datasets smaller than ~55k sessions; behaviour at a few thousand sessions is not documented.
- No replication study from 2025–2026 comparing the newest sequential models (e.g. LLM-initialised or semantic-ID models) with session-KNN was found.

## 3. Two-tower retrieval + ranking, candidate generation vs. ranking, ANN search (FAISS, HNSW, pgvector)

### Takeaway
The retrieval-then-ranking architecture exists to cut *millions* of items down to hundreds within a latency budget; with tens of thousands of products the whole catalog can be scored or the answers precomputed in batch, so a trained two-tower model and a dedicated ANN service buy nothing for a small shop. The only piece worth borrowing is "precompute item-to-item neighbours offline, serve from a lookup table".

### Cited Findings
- Eugene Yan's system-design summary: discovery systems split into offline vs. online and retrieval vs. ranking; "Candidate retrieval is a fast—but coarse—step to narrow down millions of items into hundreds of candidates"; "Ranking is a slower—but more precise—step to score and rank top candidates"; offline jobs train models, embed catalog items and build the ANN index or knowledge graph. [primary: practitioner blog] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/system-design-for-discovery](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/system-design-for-discovery/)
- Same source, on over-engineering: "think hard about whether you need real-time retrieval and ranking, or if batch recommendations will suffice"; the recommended starting point is "candidate retrieval via simple embeddings and approximate nearest neighbors", adding a ranker later. Alibaba's Taobao example precomputes an item-to-item (i2i) similarity map offline and only reads it online. [primary: practitioner blog] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/system-design-for-discovery](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/system-design-for-discovery/)
- Industrial two-tower examples and their scale/cost: Etsy unified-embedding two-tower retrieval (+2.63% site-wide conversion, FAISS with 4-bit product quantization, p99 < 20 ms, recall loss < 4%); Best Buy 50M-parameter two-tower semantic search (+3% conversion when added to lexical search, trained with LLM-generated synthetic queries); Zalando two-tower candidate generation + multi-task ranking + policy layer (+15% engagement, +2.2% revenue). All are large marketplaces with ML platform teams. [secondary summary of company papers] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/)
- Why two-tower exists: scoring every user-item pair with a complex model is infeasible at inference time, so separate user and item embeddings + ANN search are used for candidate generation; training typically relies on in-batch negatives and guides warn that too small a batch is a common pitfall ("thousands" needed for good negatives). [secondary: vendor/practitioner guides] — [Shaped.ai two-tower deep dive](https://shaped.ai/blog/the-two-tower-model-for-recommendation-systems-a-deep-dive); [machinelearningatscale.com guide](https://machinelearningatscale.com/blog/two-tower-models-industry-guide)
- pgvector performs **exact** nearest-neighbour search by default (perfect recall); ANN indexes (HNSW, IVFFlat) trade recall for speed and are optional. [primary] — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)
- Exact-scan latency data point: Neon measured sequential scans on GIST-960 (960-dim) subsets on a 4 CU / 16 GB instance: ~36 ms for 10k rows; "sequential scans start to become costly at 50k rows". [primary: vendor docs] — [Neon docs, vector search optimization](https://neon.com/docs/ai/ai-vector-search-optimization). EnterpriseDB's docs make the general claim that for tables under 1M rows a sequential scan is often fast enough (not a measurement). [secondary] — [EDB pgvector docs](https://www.enterprisedb.com/docs/pg_extensions/pgvector/using/)

### Inferences
- Scale threshold (inference, no source gives a hard number): two-tower + ANN starts to pay off when (a) the catalog is in the hundreds of thousands to millions so per-request full scoring is impossible, (b) there are enough interactions to fill large training batches (millions of events), and (c) recommendations must react to the request in real time. None of the three holds for tens of thousands of products with modest orders.
- For this shop the "retrieval" stage is a precomputed table: for each product, top-N related products from co-occurrence / session-KNN / content embeddings, written by cron to PostgreSQL or Redis. "Ranking" is a few business rules (in stock, active, different variant group, price band). This is the Alibaba i2i-map pattern at small scale.
- FAISS / a separate vector database is unnecessary at 10k–100k vectors: pgvector inside the existing PostgreSQL covers it (details in section 4), and since neighbours can be precomputed in batch, even query latency is irrelevant.

### Gaps
- No authoritative source states a numeric catalog-size or event-count threshold at which two-tower retrieval beats simpler methods; the thresholds above are inference.
- The original YouTube (Covington et al. 2016) and Google two-tower papers were not fetched in this session; their content is represented only through Eugene Yan's summary.

## 4. Content / text embeddings for cold start; multilingual models for Russian and Uzbek; pgvector limits and cost

### Takeaway
Embedding product name + category + attributes with a multilingual text model and storing vectors in pgvector is the one "modern" technique that needs no interaction data at all, costs cents to dollars for a catalog of tens of thousands of products, and directly solves cold start ("similar products", recommendations for customers with one or two purchases). Russian quality is well benchmarked (multilingual-e5-large-instruct and BGE-M3 lead among open models); for Uzbek there is essentially no public benchmark, so quality must be checked on the shop's own data.

### Cited Findings
**Models and Russian benchmarks**
- ruMTEB (SaluteDevices, 2024; 23 tasks in 7 categories) results, retrieval / STS / average: rubert-tiny2 8.9 / 61.9 / 39.1; multilingual-e5-small 65.8 / 69.5 / 56.4; multilingual-e5-base 67.1 / 70.2 / 57.1; multilingual-e5-large 74.0 / 71.6 / 60.4; **BGE-M3 74.8 / 73.7 / 60.8**; ru-en-RoSBERTa 66.5 / 74.0 / 60.4; **multilingual-e5-large-instruct 75.1 / 74.9 / 64.7**; e5-mistral-7b-instruct 74.1 / 74.4 / 64.9. Older Russian-only sentence models are far worse at retrieval (8.5–19.1). [primary] — [arXiv 2408.12503](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12503v1)
- GigaEmbeddings (Russian-focused LLM-based embedder) self-reports 69.1 on ruMTEB, top of that leaderboard as of December 2024. Self-reported. [primary, self-reported] — [arXiv 2510.22369](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.22369)
- BGE-M3: one model for 100+ languages, with dense, sparse and multi-vector retrieval modes. multilingual-e5 was evaluated on MIRACL (16 languages; mE5-large nDCG@10 66.5). [primary] — [mE5 technical report, arXiv 2402.05672](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.05672); [BGE-M3 model card mirror](https://huggingface.co/the-sk/bge-m3)
- small model option: multilingual-e5-small is only ~9 points behind the large model on ruMTEB retrieval (65.8 vs 74.0) — relevant for CPU-only inference. [primary] — [arXiv 2408.12503](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12503v1)

**Uzbek**
- No Uzbek-specific embedding benchmark or Uzbek scores for mE5 / BGE-M3 / API models were found. MMTEB (ICLR 2025) covers 500+ tasks across 250+ languages (the ICLR abstract says 1,000+), but the retrieved material does not show per-language Uzbek results. [primary, coverage only] — [MMTEB, arXiv 2502.13595](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2502.13595v2)
- A community model exists that trims multilingual-e5-large-instruct's vocabulary for Uzbek (39.73% smaller), with a warning that it may not work for other languages; no Uzbek evaluation numbers were found for it. [primary: model card] — [Hugging Face: multilingual-e5-large-instruct-uzs-32768](https://huggingface.co/alphaedge-ai/multilingual-e5-large-instruct-uzs-32768)

**Embedding API prices (2026)** — all from third-party aggregators, not verified on the vendors' own pricing pages:
- OpenAI text-embedding-3-small $0.02 per 1M tokens (1,536 dims, configurable; Batch API $0.01); text-embedding-3-large $0.13 per 1M (3,072 dims, configurable). Voyage voyage-4-lite $0.02, voyage-4-large $0.12 per 1M, with 200M free tokens. Cohere Embed v4 most often listed at $0.12 per 1M. Gemini Embedding 2 listed at $0.20 per 1M. [secondary] — [costbench embedding pricing 2026](https://costbench.com/stats/embedding-api-pricing-2026/); [OpenAI embeddings pricing summary](https://www.costbench.com/software/embedding-apis/openai-embeddings/)

**pgvector (version 0.8.7 at time of reading; Postgres 13+)**
- Limits: `vector` — up to 16,000 dims stored, **2,000 dims indexable** (same cap for HNSW and IVFFlat); `halfvec` (half precision) — **4,000 dims indexable**; `bit` — 64,000 dims indexable; `sparsevec` — 1,000 non-zero elements (HNSW). A column over the limit can be stored but `CREATE INDEX` fails. [primary] — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector); [explanation of the 2,000-dim limit](https://particula.tech/blog/pgvector-2000-dimension-index-limit)
- HNSW defaults `m=16`, `ef_construction=64`, `hnsw.ef_search=40`; no training step (can be created on an empty table); builds much faster when the graph fits in `maintenance_work_mem`; better speed/recall than IVFFlat but slower build and more memory. [primary] — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)
- IVFFlat: create after data is loaded; `lists = rows/1000` up to 1M rows; start `probes = sqrt(lists)`. [primary] — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)
- Filtering caveat: ANN indexes apply `WHERE` filters after the index scan (with a 10% match rate and `ef_search=40` only ~4 rows come back on average); iterative index scans (since 0.8.0) keep scanning until enough rows pass the filter; for selective filters a B-tree on the filter column + exact search, or partial indexes, are recommended. Relevant for "similar products that are active / in stock / in the same item category". [primary] — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)
- Size/build data point: 25,000 rows × 3,072 dims as `halfvec` — HNSW index built in 29 s, ~193 MB; IVFFlat built ~5.8× faster with nearly identical size; recommended start m=16, ef_construction=64. [secondary: DBA blog, March 2026] — [dbi-services pgvector guide](https://www.dbi-services.com/blog/pgvector-a-guide-for-dba-part-2-indexes-update-march-2026/)
- Exact search without index: ~36 ms at 10k rows (960 dims, 16 GB instance), "costly" from ~50k rows. [primary: vendor docs] — [Neon docs](https://neon.com/docs/ai/ai-vector-search-optimization)

**Content embeddings combined with behaviour (evidence that content helps cold start, at big-tech scale)**
- YouTube found dense content embeddings used directly performed worse than random-hash IDs in the ranking model, while discretised "semantic IDs" derived from them beat hashing, especially for cold-start items; Kuaishou's multimodal-embedding clusters gave +3.6% cold-start video coverage and +3.4% clicks. These are results inside large learned rankers, not standalone similar-item features. [secondary summary] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/)
- Google's managed "Similar Items" model and Algolia's "Looking Similar" model need **no user events** — catalog data only — which is the vendors' own acknowledgement that content similarity is the cold-start answer. [primary] — [Google models doc](https://docs.cloud.google.com/retail/docs/models); [Algolia Recommend overview](https://www.algolia.com/doc/guides/algolia-recommend/overview)

### Inferences
- Cost arithmetic (mine, using the secondary prices above): 50,000 products × ~200 tokens of "name + category path + brand + attributes" ≈ 10M tokens ≈ **$0.20** with a $0.02/M model or ≈ $1.3 with a $0.13/M model; re-embedding only changed products by cron costs effectively nothing. A local multilingual-e5-small/base on CPU is also feasible as a one-off batch for tens of thousands of short texts (no GPU needed), at the price of adding PyTorch / ONNX to the deployment.
- Dimension choice: 1,024-dim open models (mE5-large, BGE-M3) and 1,536-dim OpenAI small fit under pgvector's 2,000-dim index cap; 3,072-dim vectors need `halfvec` or a reduced `dimensions` setting. At 10k–100k products, 1,024-dim vectors are ~4 KB each (float4) → ~0.4 GB for 100k rows before indexing (my arithmetic).
- At this catalog size the right pattern is: compute each product's top-N nearest neighbours in a nightly job (an exact scan is fine in batch) and store them in an ordinary table / Redis; an HNSW index is only needed if neighbours are queried live (e.g. "similar to the customer's last purchases" at request time), and then HNSW with default parameters is sufficient.
- Recommendations for customers with little history: average (or recency-weight) the vectors of the products they bought/viewed and take nearest products, excluding the same variant groups — needs zero training data. But content similarity produces **substitutes** (similar items), not **complements** (what else is needed for the job); for building materials after a purchase, complements are usually the more useful recommendation, so content embeddings should feed "similar products" and cold-start fallback, with complements coming from co-purchase data or LLM-generated category-level complements (section 5).
- Uzbek risk mitigation: product names in this catalog exist in both `name_ru` and `name_uz`; embedding the Russian text (best-benchmarked language) for item-to-item similarity avoids depending on unmeasured Uzbek quality. Uzbek quality only matters for query-side use (search) and should be spot-checked manually.
- Near-duplicate SKUs (sizes, colours) will dominate text-similarity neighbours; de-duplicate by variant group and consider restricting neighbours to the same or sibling item category.

### Gaps
- No public benchmark of any embedding model on Uzbek retrieval / similarity was found; whether Uzbek is in MMTEB's task list, and with what scores, was not confirmed.
- No ruMTEB scores for commercial API models (OpenAI, Cohere, Voyage, Gemini) were retrieved; vendor pricing pages were not fetched directly — prices above are from aggregators and conflict in places (one outlier listed Cohere Embed v4 at $0.01/M).

## 5. LLM-based recommendation (2025–2026): re-ranker, complement generation, LLM features, conversational, semantic IDs / generative retrieval

### Takeaway
The practical LLM pattern, including at companies with large ML teams, is **offline**: an LLM generates complements / themes / labels / descriptions in batch, the output is mapped to and validated against the catalog, cached, and served by ordinary lookups; online LLM ranking is too slow and costly, and free-form generation produces out-of-catalog items unless grounded. Generative retrieval with semantic IDs (TIGER and successors) is research / big-tech territory that needs large interaction logs and GPUs.

### Cited Findings
**LLM as zero-shot ranker / re-ranker**
- "Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Rankers for Recommender Systems" (Hou et al., ECIR 2024): LLMs can rank candidates produced by other retrievers given the interaction history as a condition and can be competitive with conventional models when candidates come from multiple generators, but they **struggle to perceive the order of the history** and suffer from **position bias and popularity bias** (partly mitigated by prompting / bootstrapping). The paper does not provide a cost/latency analysis. [primary, via abstract-level reading] — [arXiv 2305.08845](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.08845)
- Latency/cost data point from production: at Indeed, a fine-tuned GPT-3.5 matched GPT-4 labels at about a quarter of the cost and latency, but its **6.7-second latency** was still too slow for online use, so a lightweight classifier was trained on the LLM labels for real-time filtering (results: bad matches −17.68%, unsubscribes −4.97%, applications +4.13%). [secondary summary of the company write-up] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/)
- Distillation as the standard cost lever: DLLM2Rec distils a Llama2-7B-based recommender into GRU4Rec / SASRec with an average +47.97% on HR/NDCG over the un-distilled students, cutting inference from 3–6 hours (teacher) to 1.6–1.8 seconds. Yan's caveat: several LLM-recsys papers compare against untuned baselines or only offline, so gains may be overstated. [secondary] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/)

**Hallucination / out-of-catalog items**
- Zero-shot LLMs "often produce out-of-catalog or non-existent items", and smaller models are hit hardest (Rank-GRPO, conversational recommendation, ICLR 2026). [primary, via abstract-level reading] — [arXiv 2510.20150](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.20150v3)
- RecLM ("Eliminating Out-of-Domain Recommendations in LLM-based Recommender Systems", ACL 2026 Findings, Microsoft RecAI): three grounding approaches — embedding-based retrieval, constrained generation over item titles, discrete item-tokenizer generation — each drive the out-of-domain rate to **zero** with the same backbone; constrained generation gave the best accuracy. I.e. hallucination is solved by construction (retrieve/constrain), not by prompting. [primary, via abstract-level reading] — [arXiv 2505.03336](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.03336)
- Grounding by mapping: BIGRec generates an item description and maps it to the nearest real item by embedding similarity (weighted by popularity); Google's CALRec generates next-item text and matches it to the catalog with BM25. [secondary] — [BIGRec summary](https://papers.lunadong.com/paper/10561); [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/)
- A 2025 systematic review of 138 retrieval-augmented LLM-recommendation studies (2023–2025) found only sporadic explicit treatment of cold start, hallucination and robustness — the risk is acknowledged but under-measured. [secondary, from search summary] — [GR-LLMs survey context, arXiv 2507.06507](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06507v2)
- A 2026 preprint argues that the usual binary "in-catalog rate" hides matcher error and says nothing about confidence. Not peer-reviewed; unverified beyond the abstract. [primary preprint, unverified] — [arXiv 2608.10008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10008)

**LLM-generated complements, themes and project lists (the offline pattern)**
- Instacart (search, 2024): LLMs generate complementary and substitute items for a query; the response "is parsed and mapped to relevant products in our catalog"; content is **generated offline** in batch for cost and latency; reported effect: more products added to cart per search. [primary blog; fetched only via search summary because the page returned 403] — [Instacart tech blog](https://tech.instacart.com/supercharging-discovery-in-search-with-llms-556c585d4720); [ZenML LLMOps summary](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/using-llms-to-enhance-search-discovery-and-recommendations)
- Instacart (discovery recommendations, 2025–2026): cascaded pipeline — an LLM designs personalised themed placements from purchase history, themes are mapped to **taxonomy-grounded** retrieval descriptors (taxonomy-grounded worked best for relevance), ~100 candidates are pre-selected by embedding similarity from a 300,000-term keyword corpus and a second LLM picks from that subset (RAG pruning cut generation cost 15–20%); outputs use constrained decoding / structured schemas; results are **cached for runtime retrieval**; quality is controlled with LLM-as-judge tuned against human labels, then a fine-tuned DeBERTa cross-encoder checks product–theme match at **>99% lower cost** than closed-weight LLM inference; a closed LLM is used as teacher for a smaller internal model for latency/cost. No quantitative business lift is published (A/B results described only as promising). Limitations stated: LLM judges fail on edge cases. [primary] — [Instacart: Our Early Journey to Transform Discovery Recommendations with LLMs](https://company.instacart.com/tech-innovation/our-early-journey-to-transform-instacart-s-discovery-recommendations-with-llms)
- Yelp precomputes LLM query expansions in batch and caches them in key-value stores, covering 95% of traffic. [secondary] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/)
- Home improvement, project lists: The Home Depot launched "Material List Builder AI" (January 2026) — a pro describes a project by voice/text/document and gets a draft material list organised by project phase, editable, with product recommendations populated under each section with the pro's pricing and stock availability. A press release, no quality metrics. [primary: company press release] — [Home Depot newsroom](https://corporate.homedepot.com/news/company/home-depot-launches-ai-powered-material-lists-help-pros-save-time-building-complete); [IR release](https://ir.homedepot.com/news-releases/2026/01-26-2026-130100894)
- Lowe's assistant "Mylow": an independent hands-on test found product recommendations accurate per project but **incomplete** (for deck staining it suggested paint rollers but not a roller frame or tray). [secondary: consumer review] — [BobVila.com](https://www.bobvila.com/diy/ai-tools.md)
- Amazon Science (ECCR) motivates LLM complements with the observation that items frequently bought together are not necessarily true complements, and fine-tunes an LLM to produce complements with explanations (research paper, not a production report). [primary: research] — [Amazon Science: ECCR](https://amazon.science/publications/eccr-explainable-and-coherent-complement-recommendation-based-on-large-language-models)
- LLM as labeller of complement pairs: a 2025 study reports GPT-4o-mini reaching consistency 0.989 and classification accuracy 0.849 on complementary-relation labels at a fraction of human annotation cost. Attribution of these figures to the specific paper is from a search summary and was not verified. [secondary, unverified] — [arXiv 2507.03945 (Function-based Labels for Complementary Recommendation)](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.03945v1)

**LLM-generated item text / attributes as features**
- Bing: GPT-4 wrote titles/snippets for 2M pages, distilled into Mistral-7B and a small cross-encoder (clickbait −31%, duplicates −76%); Amazon playlists: LLM-written descriptions + synthetic queries gave "double-digit" recall gains; Walmart uses LLMs for catalog attribute extraction and validation at scale. [secondary / primary blog] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/); [Walmart Global Tech](https://tech.walmart.com/content/walmart-global-tech/en_us/blog/post/using-llms-to-manage-product-catalogs.html)

**Semantic IDs / generative retrieval**
- TIGER ("Recommender Systems with Generative Retrieval", Rajput et al., NeurIPS 2023): each item gets a Semantic ID (tuple of codewords from quantised content embeddings); a Transformer seq2seq model reads the Semantic IDs of the session's items and generates the next item's Semantic ID; claims to significantly outperform SOTA baselines on its datasets and to improve retrieval of items with no interaction history. [primary] — [arXiv 2305.05065](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05065)
- Successors are industrial-scale: Kuaishou's OneRec builds on semantic-ID encoding; the GR-LLMs survey (2025) covers this line. Spotify's joint generative retrieval for search + recommendation gained +16% recall@30 on average, yet "generative models still trail specialized baselines overall". LinkedIn's 360Brew is a 150B-parameter decoder trained on 256–512 H100 GPUs. [secondary] — [GR-LLMs survey, arXiv 2507.06507](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06507v2); [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/)

### Inferences
- **Practical for a small shop (no GPU, cron):** batch-generate complements at the **item-category level**, not per SKU: for each of the shop's item categories ask an LLM "which other categories from this list are needed to complete the job done with X?", passing the shop's own category list in the prompt and forcing a structured output of category IDs (closed-set choice = grounding by construction, the same principle as RecLM's constrained generation and Instacart's taxonomy-grounded descriptors). Then: (1) drop any ID not in the catalog, (2) keep only categories that have active in-stock products, (3) optionally confirm against real co-purchase counts where data exists, (4) have a content manager review the table once — a few hundred to a few thousand category pairs is reviewable by a human. At serving time pick concrete products inside the complement categories by popularity / stock / price. This is a one-off or monthly job of some thousands of LLM calls; hallucinated products are impossible because the LLM never names products.
- This pattern is the best fit for the shop's actual weakness — little co-purchase data — because the LLM contributes world knowledge about construction projects (tile → adhesive, grout, spacers, trowel) that sparse order history cannot reveal; the Mylow review shows the failure mode to check for is **incompleteness**, not only wrong items.
- Per-product LLM calls (tens of thousands of SKUs) are affordable in batch but mostly redundant with the category-level table; per-request LLM calls (re-ranking, conversational) add seconds of latency (Indeed: 6.7 s even after distillation) and per-request cost, and need a retrieval step to stay in-catalog — not justified for a shop without ML staff.
- "LLM-generated attributes as features" is already half-done in this codebase conceptually (1C free-form characteristics → structured attributes); an LLM could normalise names/attributes in batch, which improves text embeddings and filters, but that is catalog-quality work rather than recommendation.
- TIGER / semantic-ID generative retrieval, fine-tuned LLM recommenders, RL-trained conversational recommenders: "needs big data and an ML team" — all evidence comes from big-tech datasets or academic benchmarks with GPU training.
- Russian/Uzbek: complements generated as category IDs are language-independent; any customer-facing LLM text (explanations, "for this project you also need…") would need review in both languages, and no evidence on LLM quality for Uzbek product text was collected here.

### Gaps
- No published, quantified A/B result was found for "LLM-generated complements, precomputed offline" in a small or mid-size shop; Instacart reports direction only (more add-to-carts per search; discovery results "promising").
- No published measurement of out-of-catalog rates for current (2026) frontier LLMs on a closed product list was retrieved in a form that could be quoted with numbers; RecLM's and Rank-GRPO's tables were read only at abstract level.
- No LLM pricing was collected in this research, so the cost of a batch complement job is not quantified here.
- No engineering write-up from a home-improvement retailer describing the architecture or accuracy of project-based / complement recommendations was found (Home Depot: press release and a conference-talk mention only; Lowe's: third-party review only).
- The Instacart search post (2024) returned HTTP 403; its details come from a search-result summary and a third-party (ZenML) digest.

## 7. Order of adoption recommended by practitioners (heuristics → co-occurrence → embeddings → learned models)

### Takeaway
Practitioner guidance is consistent: launch with non-ML heuristics and instrument everything first, move to simple statistics/models once there is data, and add complexity only when simple tricks stop paying — and the academic replication literature shows that, in recommendation specifically, the "simple" tier is often not even beaten by the complex one. For this shop the ordering maps cleanly to: popularity/category heuristics → co-purchase + session-KNN → content embeddings in pgvector (and offline LLM category complements) → learned models or a managed service only if event volume grows by orders of magnitude.

### Cited Findings
- Google "Rules of Machine Learning" (Zinkevich): Rule #1 "Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning"; Rule #2 "First, design and implement metrics" — "track as much as possible in your current system"; Rule #3 "Choose machine learning over a complex heuristic" — "A simple heuristic can get your product out the door. A complex heuristic is unmaintainable"; Rule #4 "Keep the first model simple and get the infrastructure right" — "The first model provides the biggest boost to your product, so it doesn't need to be fancy"; Rule #7 "Turn heuristics into features, or handle them externally"; Phase III: gains diminish and only then does the ML need to become sophisticated. [primary] — [Google Rules of ML](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml)
- Eugene Yan, "The First Rule of Machine Learning: Start without Machine Learning": for recommendations, start with "top-performing items from the previous period", optionally "segmented by categories (e.g., genres, brands)", then aggregated co-interaction statistics for item-to-item similarity (Alibaba's Swing as an example); move to ML when the non-ML baseline is established and maintaining rules costs more than building a model, and only when data pipelines and labels are reliable. Quotes practitioners (Boykis, Husain, Laiacano) giving the same advice. [primary: practitioner blog] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/first-rule-of-ml](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/first-rule-of-ml/)
- Eugene Yan, system design: "think hard about whether you need real-time retrieval and ranking, or if batch recommendations will suffice"; start with "simple embeddings and approximate nearest neighbors", add ranking later. [primary: practitioner blog] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/system-design-for-discovery](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/system-design-for-discovery/)
- Empirical support for stopping at the simple tier: nearest-neighbour session methods preferable to neural ones "in most cases" (Ludewig et al.); kNN and sequential rules beat all eight GNN models on MRR (Shehzad & Jannach 2024); 6 of 7 reproducible neural top-n recommenders often beaten by simple heuristics (Dacrema et al. 2019); BERT4Rec's reported edge over SASRec traced to the loss function (Klenitskiy & Vasilev 2023). [primary] — [arXiv 1910.12781](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12781); [arXiv 2312.16695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16695); [arXiv 1907.06902](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06902); [arXiv 2309.07602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07602)
- Evaluation lesson from home improvement: offline metrics gave no clear winner and disagreed with human experts, largely because near-identical products with different IDs are not credited — expert review of recommendation lists was added before choosing. [primary: abstract] — [Kouki et al., RecSys 2020](https://relational.ai/blog/from-the-lab-to-production-a-case-study-of-session-based-recommendations-in-the-home-improvement)
- Even large companies run LLM/neural components offline and cache results (Yelp: 95% of traffic from cached LLM output; Instacart: placements and keywords cached for runtime retrieval; Alibaba: precomputed i2i map). [secondary / primary] — [eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/recsys-llm/); [Instacart](https://company.instacart.com/tech-innovation/our-early-journey-to-transform-instacart-s-discovery-recommendations-with-llms); [eugeneyan.com/writing/system-design-for-discovery](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/system-design-for-discovery/)

### Inferences
Suggested adoption ladder for this shop, with what each rung needs (synthesis of the sections above):

| Rung | Method | Data needed | Infra needed | Evidence vs. simpler rung on small data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Instrument: log product views, cart adds, purchases with session/customer IDs; define the metric (add-to-cart from recommendation blocks, attach rate) | — | PostgreSQL table | Rules of ML #2 — prerequisite for everything else |
| 1 | Popularity heuristics per item category / brand, "bought again", recently viewed | Orders only | SQL + cron + Redis | Baseline; managed services fall back to it too |
| 2 | Co-purchase counts and session-KNN / sequential rules ("after X people view/buy Y") | Thousands of multi-item orders / tens of thousands of sessions | SQL/Python cron job | Strong: kNN/rules match or beat neural session models on 55k–60k-session datasets |
| 3a | Text embeddings of products in pgvector for "similar products" and cold-start customers | None (catalog text only) | pgvector extension + one-off embedding batch (API cents, or CPU model) | No small-shop A/B evidence; vendors' no-event "similar items" models are the same idea |
| 3b | Offline LLM-generated category-level complements, validated against catalog and by a human | None (category list only) | Batch LLM calls by cron | Directional evidence only (Instacart); solves the sparse co-purchase problem |
| 4 | item2vec / Meta-Prod2Vec on sessions, blended with co-occurrence | Hundreds of thousands of sessions for tail benefit | CPU training job | Helps only on tail / unseen pairs; co-occurrence wins on frequent pairs |
| 5 | Managed service (Recombee, Personalize batch) or Gorse | Formal minimum ~1,000 interactions; meaningful only with much more | External SaaS or extra service | Unproven for small shops; costs $99–$2,000/month scale |
| 6 | SASRec-type sequential models, two-tower retrieval, semantic-ID generative retrieval, online LLM ranking | Millions of interactions | GPUs, ML engineers, feature/ANN serving | Not shown to beat rungs 2–3 on small data |

- Rungs 3a/3b are placed before learned collaborative embeddings because they need **no interaction data** — the shop's scarcest resource — whereas rung 4+ only becomes better than rung 2 as data grows.
- The step from rung 2/3 upward should be decided by the instrumented metric from rung 0 (A/B or before/after on recommendation-block add-to-cart rate), not by offline accuracy, given the Home Depot finding that offline metrics mislead in catalogs with many near-duplicate SKUs.
